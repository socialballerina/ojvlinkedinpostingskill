Silicon Valley is supposed to be the world's innovation capital. WIPO's 2026 index ranked a Chinese corridor first.

Shenzhen, Hong Kong and Guangzhou, counted as one science and technology cluster. Tokyo and Yokohama came second. San Jose and San Francisco came third.

That is not a ranking story. It is a distance story.

From a desk in London the table invites an argument about methodology. The more useful question is what the cluster is made of.

Per million inhabitants over five years, WIPO counted 2,259 international patent applications, 4,060 scientific articles and 138 venture capital deals in that corridor. China hosted 25 of the world's 100 biggest clusters. The United States hosted 20.

On the ground the three cities work as one place. A paper written in Hong Kong, a prototype turned in Shenzhen, a supplier across the river in Guangzhou, all inside a day's travel. The Loop Hong Kong Park, on the Shenzhen border, opened in December 2025 and had more than 100 companies and institutions signed up by September 2026.

The ecosystem is the product, not any one city. You can read the ranking anywhere. You only understand the distances by crossing them.
