China installed 295,000 industrial robots in 2024. The rest of the world managed 247,000 between them.

You can subsidise a factory. You cannot subsidise a habit.

Read from a desk in London, that number looks like industrial policy, and the comfortable conclusion is that it reverts when the subsidy stops.

Here is the part that does not revert. China's operational robot stock passed 2 million units in 2024, the largest of any country, and Chinese manufacturers now hold 57% of their own domestic robot market.

That second number is the one to sit with. The buyers stopped importing the tools.

On the ground this shows up as a different kind of conversation. Ask a plant manager in the Pearl River Delta what they automated last and you rarely get a strategy answer. You get a payback period, in months, and the name of a supplier two hours away who will modify the cell for you.

The ecosystem is the product, not the company. A robot you can get serviced on Thursday beats a better robot you cannot.

Which part of your line would you automate first if the payback were under a year?

Would you bet against Chinese biotech because you cannot name a single Chinese drug brand?

You will not find the brand, because the brand was never the plan.

Out-licensing deals from the greater China region rose nearly tenfold from 2021 to reach $137.7 billion in 2025. By September 2026 the average deal size had already hit $1.3 billion, up 76% on 2025, and the average upfront fee was $77.7 million, double the year before.

The mechanism is the part worth studying. These companies are not building American salesforces. They are selling the asset to a partner that already has one, and taking the upfront instead of the brand.

AstraZeneca's weight-loss agreement with CSPC Pharmaceutical is worth up to $18.5 billion. AbbVie's deal with RemeGen runs to $5.6 billion.

This is what founders in Asia keep missing about going west. The hard part is rarely the product. It is the buyer, the pricing and the story, and there is more than one way to solve for all three. Sometimes the answer is not to enter the market. It is to sell to whoever already has.

We had nothing to do with these deals. We just think it is the clearest example around.

What would you give up to reach the buyer two years earlier?

India is supposed to be where Western retail goes to die. IKEA's answer was to rebuild the price, not the marketing.

When it opened its first Indian store in 2018, the range carried 1,000 products under 200 rupees and 500 under 100 rupees.

That was not a launch promotion. It was a supply chain decision.

Indian rules require a foreign single-brand retailer to source 30% of its materials locally within five years. IKEA treated the requirement as the strategy rather than the obstacle. It moved local sourcing from single digits to 20 to 25%, and by 2020 was saying that sourcing wood locally, including sheesham and bamboo, would let it cut prices by a third. More than a third of the products under those lower prices were made in India.

Most Western teams run this in the opposite order. Land at the home-market price, discover the buyer will not pay it, discount into a margin hole, then conclude the market is difficult.

Price has to be rebuilt from the local cost base up, before launch, not defended after it. That is a large part of what our GTM advisory work actually is.

We have never worked with IKEA. This is commentary from the outside.

Where does your pricing break when you cross a border?

Brian Chesky was raising $150,000 at a $1.5 million valuation. Of the seven he approached, five passed and two never replied at all. He published the rejection emails himself, with the names removed.

The reasons were not insults. They were mandate. Not in our area of focus. Not the right opportunity. A long shot for our involvement. One said to come back at Series A.

That is the lesson, and founders hate it. Those investors were not wrong about their own funds. They were answering a different question from the one Chesky thought he was asking.

Seven nos from seven people who do not fund your stage or your category is not information about your company. It is information about your list.

On a raise we cut the list hard and spend the time on warm paths into funds that have actually written cheques into this shape of company recently. A shorter, better-targeted list beats a longer one. We can change who hears the pitch and how it lands, not the answer.

The post1285 chars · band 900 to 1300
Starbucks bought control of its China business, then sold it again. Not by accident, but by design.

It entered East China with regional partners, Uni-President Enterprises and President Chain Store, and let them build it.

In July 2017 it paid around $1.3 billion in cash for the remaining half of that business, the largest acquisition in its history at the time, taking full ownership of roughly 2,800 stores in China. In the same transaction it handed those partners its 50% of Taiwan for about $175 million.

In November 2025 it went the other way. Boyu Capital took up to 60% of the China retail business for $4 billion, Starbucks kept 40% and the brand licence, and the stated plan is to go from 8,000 stores to 20,000.

Ownership was never the goal. It was a dial.

Partner in to learn a market. Buy in when control is the scarce thing. Sell control when a local operator moves faster than you can and speed becomes the scarce thing.

APAC is not one market. It is 15+ ecosystems with different rules and different buyers, and the ownership structure is one of the things you recalibrate.

We were not involved in any of this. Commentary only.

Now read the growth rates rather than the sizes. The channel that built the company is the slowest growing thing in it.

That is the play, run in the right order. Use a marketplace to reach a Western buyer without a Western brand, salesforce, distributor or inch of shelf space. Then use the volume you built to earn the shelf space. Then move the customer onto a channel you own.

Most Asian companies going west try to start at step three, because owning the customer feels like the real business. It is, eventually. It is a terrible place to begin, because nobody has heard of you yet.

The hard part going west is rarely the product. It is the buyer, the pricing and the story.
